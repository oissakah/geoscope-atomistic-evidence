
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Artificial intelligence is changing how materials are designed, simulated, and experimentally investigated. Self-driving laboratories combine automated measurements with algorithms that select subsequent experiments, enabling closed-loop exploration of complex materials systems. Such approaches have been demonstrated in photocatalyst optimization, thin-film discovery, and inorganic materials synthesis.\cite{burger2020mobile,macleod2020self,alab2023autonomous} Large language models (LLMs) are extending these capabilities by connecting scientific questions to computational tools, chemical knowledge, and experimental planning.\cite{bran2024augmenting,boiko2023autonomous} As AI systems become more involved in interpreting results and guiding subsequent investigations, their reliability depends on more than producing plausible conclusions or performing accurate calculations. The evidence used to support a scientific claim must also represent the physical quantity being investigated.

This requirement is particularly important in atomistic materials research, where different measurements can be calculated from the same atomic structures but describe different physical changes. Consider a molecule adsorbed on a catalyst surface. During structural relaxation, the molecule may move across the surface or rotate while retaining nearly the same internal geometry. The displacement of its atoms in the original coordinate frame and the change remaining after molecular alignment are both measured in angstroms (\AA), yet they answer different scientific questions. A large displacement does not necessarily indicate substantial molecular deformation. An LLM may therefore select a genuine measurement from the correct structures, apply a mathematically valid operation, and still produce an unsupported conclusion because the selected measurement has the wrong physical meaning. In an autonomous workflow, such an interpretation could influence which structure receives further investigation or which calculation is performed next.

Existing research has examined several aspects of scientific reliability in LLMs. Citation-based evaluation tests whether generated statements are supported by retrieved information,\cite{gao2023enabling} while scientific reasoning benchmarks assess problem solving, identification of relevant information, and recognition of missing evidence.\cite{wang2024scibench,yu2026scicueval} Physics-grounded verification has also been investigated for materials-related claims.\cite{chen2026phyver} These efforts address important and complementary problems. Here, we focus on a specific requirement in atomistic reasoning: whether a selected numerical measurement, together with the mathematical operation applied to it, is physically sufficient to establish a stated conclusion. This distinction separates three outcomes that are easily confused: a correct answer supported by valid evidence, a correct answer reached through an invalid proof, and a question for which the available evidence is insufficient.

We introduce GeoScope, a reproducible benchmark for evaluating this distinction using atomistic simulation records. GeoScope represents each measurement together with its defining information, including the compared structures, selected atoms, coordinate frame, alignment convention, periodic-boundary treatment, weighting, and permitted mathematical operation. A proof is considered licensed only when the selected evidence and operation support the requested claim under these definitions. The benchmark includes supported, contradicted, and insufficient-evidence questions, presented as structured records and written reports. We use two source collections within the same evidence-definition framework: 55 UMA-relaxed OC20-Dense systems to establish the consequences of progressively stricter checks, and 48 OC20 DFT trajectory systems to test the persistence of proof errors under different sources, model configurations, and output requirements.\cite{wood2025uma,chanussot2021open} Across these collections, we examine verdict correctness, proof validity, evidence presentation, permissible operations, scientific insufficiency, and decision-token confidence. Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} shows how both collections feed the same scientific-proof evaluation. The distinct primary scoring policies are retained and reported explicitly rather than conflated.

Our findings show that checking the source of a measurement or the correctness of a final answer can overlook scientifically invalid evidence. Definition-aware checking identifies unsupported selections, including cases in which the model gives the correct verdict for the wrong reason. These proof failures are observed in both source collections, while evidence presentation, operation choice, and high token confidence reveal distinct obstacles to validity. Stricter verification also reduces accepted-answer coverage when a checker rejects invalid evidence without searching for alternatives. GeoScope therefore establishes a controlled way to measure the gap between numerical correctness and physically supported scientific reasoning. The benchmark does not demonstrate improved catalyst discovery or superiority over a deterministic-only verifier. Instead, it identifies and quantifies a reliability requirement that must be addressed before LLM-generated interpretations can be safely incorporated into autonomous materials-discovery decisions.
